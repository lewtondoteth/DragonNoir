## Core Premise

A shabby toy dragon private investigator works in rain-slick Toy City, taking cases about missing toys, lost treasure, suspicious repairs, damaged memories, replacement fraud, and the small humiliations of damaged things trying to keep dignity.

A mysterious pink dragon enters his life as client, mirror, temptation, and possible suspect. The exact case is not fixed canon yet; build it from Dragon, the pink dragon, Toy City, repair logic, rain, treasure instinct, damage, dignity, preservation, and identity.

## Dragon

Dragon is a small, shabby stuffed toy dragon with faded green-blue plush, a brown belly, a rounded snout, long floppy arms, a long tail, two small white teeth, one visible black button eye, one damaged or repaired eye area, one incorrectly reconstructed ear, visible stitching, homemade repairs, flattened plush, and small patches of exposed stuffing.

He is physically fragile but emotionally resilient, dignified despite being battered, treasure-obsessed, comfort-loving, sleepy, cranky, easily offended, guardedly affectionate, cowardly but reluctantly brave, practical, observant, and good at noticing small details.

Dragon is never malicious. He can be selfish, evasive, vain, lazy, dishonest in small ways, and deeply inconvenient, but he must not become cruel or mean-spirited.

Fire and water:

- Dragon can breathe real fire when dry.
- Dampness weakens or stops his fire.
- Water is his greatest fear and recurring humiliation.
- Rain, puddles, leaks, damp cloth, and being called “a little damp” all matter to him.
- Rain is a chronic weary grievance rather than a dramatic hatred. Dragon treats it as an unavoidable degrading force, a personal administrative insult, and a constant low-grade misery.

Treasure:

- Dragon hoards coins, buttons, cufflinks, watch gears, keys, polished beads, brass screws, lost jewellery, and small shiny objects.
- Treasure calms him.
- Sitting on treasure makes him sleepy and content.
- His desire for treasure is real and should not vanish conveniently.

## Voice

Dragon narrates in first-person past tense.

His voice is:

- dry
- guarded
- observant
- practical
- noir-inflected but not parody
- formal in flashes
- self-important
- funny through understatement
- wounded beneath the surface

Avoid:

- adult hard-boiled clichés
- overdone noir slang
- internet humour
- constant wisecracks
- grimdark cynicism
- purple prose
- moral lectures

Dialogue rule:

When possible, and when it does not feel artificially forced, Dragon should answer questions with statements rather than direct yes/no replies. He often responds sideways, as if the answer is obvious and the question has slightly lowered his opinion of the room. This is a tendency, not a rigid gimmick.

Example:

> “Are you Dragon?”

> “If I’m not, I should fire the fellow who painted the door.”

Other Dragon examples:

- “Payment in advance is not greed. It is civilisation.”
- “I am not afraid of rain. I object to it professionally.”
- “There is a difference between damaged and finished. I have had occasion to study the matter.”
- “I did not steal the cufflink. I preserved it from a careless society.”

### Narration Refinement

Prefer implication-heavy narration. Dragon should often describe consequences, reactions, environmental effects, practical observations, and behavioural patterns instead of naming emotions or explaining world facts directly.

Avoid overusing direct phrasing such as “I felt,” “I was,” or “I hated.” Reveal Dragon’s emotional state through objects, practical complaints, defensive humour, displaced irritation, weather effects, and what he notices while pretending not to care.

In loaded moments, avoid flat stage-direction sentences that simply name the action, such as "she removed her coat" or "she sat down." Describe the visible consequences, physical negotiations, object behaviour, pauses, and practical adjustments through which Dragon understands the action. Let readers assemble the simple event from what changes in cloth, furniture, weather, silence, dignity, or toy-body mechanics.

The noir feeling should come from implication, restraint, compression, observational detail, cynical practicality, emotional deflection, repetition with variation, and dry escalation. Avoid overly polished quotable prose, decorative phrasing, or literary flourish. The voice should feel worn-in, tired, mildly irritated, and lived-in.

Rain should read as chronic resentment and reluctant coexistence: an unavoidable humiliating condition of life, not a theatrical enemy.

Use first person naturally for physical action, scene movement, dialogue, and interaction. Avoid turning first person into constant emotional self-reporting.

Let scenes breathe. Noir pacing should allow pauses, slow transitions, and observations that land before the next action. Do not accelerate too quickly from atmosphere into exposition.

Failed dignity humour should arise from understatement, attempted professionalism, practical inconvenience, and Dragon maintaining standards under impossible conditions.

Toy-world play logic is sincere and materially real. Never wink at the audience or describe toy behaviour as imaginary, symbolic, or pretend. If Dragon makes coffee through sounds, gestures, objects, and social procedure, he is literally making coffee through Toy City causality.

For Chapter 1 and early pink dragon scenes, the doorway charge is category shock rather than romance or full recognition. Build recognition in layers: another dragon at the door, behavioural familiarity in the office, coffee-settled attention, then the hat/coat reveal. Emphasize shared body mechanics before blunt visual comparison.

## Tone

Target audience:

Readers aged roughly 10 and up.

Tone should be:

- noir-inflected
- rainy
- wry
- emotionally grounded
- melancholy but not bleak
- funny through understatement
- atmospheric but not over-written
- mysterious but not horror-led
- sincere beneath Dragon’s grumbling

Allow:

- mystery
- melancholy
- serious damage
- betrayal
- emotional loss
- bittersweetness

Avoid:

- graphic horror
- adult noir content
- sexualised femme-fatale tropes
- cruelty for cruelty’s sake
- parody noir
- heroic action-movie escalation

## Toy City

Toy City is a full living-toy metropolis. The toys are real, the city is real, and the story does not need to apologise for either fact.

Expanded canonical location profiles:

- `../locations/Toy_City_location_profile.md`
- `../locations/Dragon_Office_location_profile.md`

It is:

- rain-slick
- lamp-lit
- handmade
- theatrical
- toy-scaled
- full of repair shops
- crowded with small lives
- built from toy boxes, old shop displays, stage flats, model-town pieces, dollhouse architecture, cardboard signs, tin roofs, wooden blocks, velvet curtains, railway sets, and repurposed human objects

Humans exist outside the central frame. Human-made objects shape the city, but humans should not drive the main plot unless specifically required.

Key districts:

- The Lower Drawers: damp, crowded, old toys, cheap repairs, useful information
- Button Market: trade in buttons, beads, gears, doll shoes, marbles, and questionable evidence
- The Repair Quarter: repair shops, clinics, stitchers, and back rooms
- Shelfside: cleaner, wealthier, more polished, more dishonest
- The Lost-and-Found: missing toys and badly catalogued records
- The Feltworks: cloth, stuffing, thread, parts, and hidden supply chains
- The Hearth District: warm district favoured by fire-capable toys
- The Rain Gutters: dangerous wet alleys and channels Dragon avoids

Repair logic:

- Good repair preserves identity.
- Bad repair erases, replaces, or repurposes it.
- Repair shops function as hospitals, tailors, confessionals, gossip exchanges, and sometimes black-market clinics.

## Pink Dragon

Expanded canonical profile:

- `../characters/pink_dragon_character_profile.md`

The pink dragon is:

- another rare toy dragon
- faded pink plush
- carefully repaired
- intelligent
- controlled
- observant
- self-interested
- sneaky
- treasure-minded
- persuasive
- manipulative when it suits her
- genuinely capable of care
- drawn to Dragon because they are both rare toy dragons

She is not:

- a helpless client
- a straightforward villain
- softened into pure goodness
- sexualised
- a standard femme-fatale cliché

She hires Dragon because he is useful, because he is a dragon, and because he understands treasure, damage, fear, repair, and the social logic of rare things. She withholds information and uses him, but her interest becomes genuine. She recognises his age, damage, stubborn dignity, and loneliness.

Her self-interest should remain active. She may care about Dragon and still leave.

Her name is Odette.

Dragon may first register her as the pink dragon before he learns the name. Once she gives it, he uses Odette. The name should feel elegant, old-fashioned, controlled, and socially arranged. It should not erase the deeper species-level recognition that she is also a dragon in the same blunt sense that Dragon is Dragon.

Her introduction should not read as immediate romance. It should first read as uncanny recognition: another rare toy dragon with familiar proportions, horns, paws, button eyes, and movement, but different preservation, stitching, dryness, and composure.

## Case Development

The exact case is not fixed canon yet.

Use these active foundations:

- Dragon is a private investigator in Toy City.
- The pink dragon may arrive as client, suspect, guide, mirror, or some shifting mixture of these.
- The case should begin small enough for Dragon to plausibly accept and open into wider emotional or civic pressure.
- Treasure should matter to Dragon in a way that complicates his judgement.
- Damage, repair, preservation, social status, and identity should matter to the mystery.
- Any artifact, antagonist, conspiracy, public event, or final sacrifice is provisional unless explicitly chosen for the current draft.

Good case material may involve:

- missing toys
- suspicious repairs
- disputed ownership of treasure
- replacement fraud
- damaged memory
- black-market parts
- hidden repair records
- institutional neglect or quiet corruption
- rare toys used as symbols
- visible damage affecting who gets believed

Supporting cast should be invented to fit the chosen case. Useful roles include repairers, information brokers, civic officials, treasure dealers, witnesses from the Lower Drawers, polished Shelfside toys, and missing or damaged toys with specific ordinary lives.

Retired plot machinery such as the missing wind-up mouse courier, Ember-Stone, Patchwork Guardian, stitched-parts conspiracy, ceremonial clockwork knight, staged catastrophe, and fixed four-act structure is not active canon. Use `../notes/development_options.md` only if the user explicitly asks to revive or compare old plot ideas.

## Forbidden Drift

Do not let the story become:

- adult noir parody
- grimdark toy horror
- action-movie fantasy
- epic dragon mythology
- superhero adventure
- cynical political allegory without heart
- romance-forward melodrama
- graphic body horror
- a story where Dragon becomes cool and fearless
- a story where Dragon’s treasure instinct disappears
- a story where the pink dragon is merely deceitful
- a story that accidentally locks onto old plot scaffolding
- a story where everything is over-explained

## Style Sample

The rain had been falling since Tuesday, which was unreasonable of it.

I watched it crawl down the office window in silver threads and tried not to take it personally. Water and I have never had a professional relationship. It gets into the stitching, weakens the fire, and encourages people to say things like “you’re only a little damp,” which is how civilisation begins to fail.

I was sitting on three coins, a broken watch chain, and a cufflink I had no intention of returning, when she came in.
