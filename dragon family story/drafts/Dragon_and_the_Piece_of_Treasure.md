# Dragon and the Piece of Treasure

Once upon a time, in the ordinary suburban Dublin house where Dragon lived with Mum, Dad, Addin, Bob, Biffin, and a dog who had never fully understood the difference between affection and demolition, there was a pile of clothes on the floor of the children’s bedroom.

This was not unusual. There are some houses in which clothes live in drawers. There are others in which clothes live briefly in drawers, then migrate to chairs, beds, bannisters, floors, and the backs of doors. The family house belonged to the second sort, though Mum maintained that this was not a natural classification and could be corrected with sufficient effort from other people.

Dragon approved of the pile.

It was high enough to feel safe from the dog, soft enough to sink into, and composed largely of Addin’s jumpers, which Dragon considered one of the better household fabrics. He had been asleep in it since after breakfast, when Bob had put him there while looking for a red sock and then forgotten both the sock and Dragon. This was not an insult. Bob forgot things with such fairness that no one could reasonably take it personally.

Dragon woke because something hard was pressing into his brown fabric belly.

At first he assumed it was a button. Then he shifted, with the careful dignity of a creature who had once been repaired under the left arm with thread of an unsuitable colour, and found a small round shining thing beneath him.

It was not a coin. Dragon knew coins. It was not a marble. Dragon knew marbles too, particularly the blue one Mum had once extracted from his ear after a week of unexplained rattling. This object was flatter than a marble and grander than a button. It had a gold edge, a scratched glass face, and a little hand inside that no longer pointed anywhere useful.

Dragon stared at it.

“Treasure,” he said.

It is important to spell this word correctly when talking to people. It is less important when one is a dragon discovering it under laundry. Dragon, being a dragon of practical instincts, did not spell it at all. He simply gathered the object beneath his stomach and became sleepy at once.

A dragon sitting on treasure is a different creature from a dragon merely sitting. The posture changes. The eyelid lowers. The tail becomes possessive. If there had been a small sign available, Dragon would have hung it nearby saying, Important Ancient Hoard. Do Not Wash.

Unfortunately, Mum came in carrying a laundry basket.

“Right,” she said. “Clothes.”

Dragon froze.

This was not a successful method of concealment, as he was two feet tall, faded green-blue, and lying in the middle of the evidence. But it was a traditional one.

“Dragon,” said Mum.

“I am not here,” said Dragon, in a low grumble.

“You are in the laundry.”

“I am in a private residence.”

“You are on Bob’s trousers.”

Dragon looked beneath one elbow. “They have not complained.”

Mum picked up the top half of the pile. Dragon pressed himself flatter over the treasure.

Mum paused.

“What are you sitting on?”

“Nothing of significance.”

“That usually means a key, a teaspoon, or Dad’s cufflink.”

Dragon lifted his chin with injured grandeur. “I would never sit on Dad’s cufflink in a heap of trousers. I have standards.”

Mum put out her hand.

Dragon made a small puffing noise. A thread of smoke came from one nostril, which might have been more impressive if it had not immediately curled round and made him sneeze.

“Dragon.”

There are many tones in which a mother can say a name. This one meant that the legal position of all treasure had suddenly become uncertain.

Dragon considered his options. He could surrender the treasure, which was unthinkable. He could argue, which was usually pleasant but rarely final. Or he could remove himself and the treasure to a safer sleeping place.

He chose the dignified retreat. It involved standing up too quickly, slipping on a pair of pyjama bottoms, and sliding down the clothes pile backwards with the treasure clutched under one arm.

Mum watched him go.

“I’ll want that back if it belongs to someone.”

“It belongs to history,” said Dragon.

“It looks like the back off an old watch.”

“History is often smaller than people expect.”

Dragon carried the treasure down the landing, past the parents’ bedroom, and into Addin and Bob’s room again, because he had panicked and come out the wrong way. This damaged the dignity of the retreat but not its purpose, since Mum was still by the laundry basket and the treasure remained under his arm.

Addin was sitting on his bed reading. He looked up calmly.

“Have you found something?”

“No,” said Dragon.

Addin looked at the gold-edged thing sticking out from under Dragon’s elbow.

“All right.”

This was one of the reasons Dragon preferred Addin. Addin often allowed facts to remain where Dragon had put them, even when they were plainly visible.

“I require a sleeping place,” Dragon said. “A settled one. Elevated. Dry. Unsuitable for dogs. Not subject to laundering.”

Addin considered this.

“Top of the bookcase?”

Dragon looked at the bookcase. It was high, warm, and cluttered with exactly the sort of objects that implied nobody would dust it before tea. It was an excellent suggestion.

Addin helped him up.

Dragon arranged himself on a stack of old comics, placed the treasure beneath his belly, tucked his tail around it, and sighed. For seven full minutes the world was properly organised.

Then Dad came in with a screwdriver.

“Ah,” said Dad. “There it is.”

Dragon opened one eye.

“What,” he said carefully, “is it?”

“The little cover from the travel alarm clock. I’ve been meaning to fix it.”

Dragon did not move.

Dad looked at Dragon. Dragon looked at Dad. The treasure, being an object of excellent judgement, remained silent.

“I am currently using it,” said Dragon.

“I can see that.”

“As treasure.”

“Yes.”

“It is performing well.”

Dad, who understood comfort and occasionally respected poor decisions if they were restful, scratched his head.

“I only need it for a minute.”

“A minute,” said Dragon, “is what people say when they intend to dismantle civilisation.”

Dad reached up.

Dragon clutched the treasure and rolled away, knocking two comics, a pencil case, and a small plastic dinosaur off the bookcase. The dinosaur landed on the dog, who had been passing by in the hallway with no plan beyond investigating air.

The dog wagged.

Dragon saw the wagging from above and understood at once that conditions had become unsuitable.

The dog’s name was Murphy, though Dragon privately referred to him as The Incident. Murphy liked Dragon. This was the difficulty. Hostility could be guarded against. Love required upholstery repairs.

Murphy put his front paws on the bed and gazed up at Dragon with bright interest.

“No,” said Dragon.

Murphy wagged harder.

“No in a broader sense.”

Murphy barked once, happily.

The bookcase was no longer elevated enough. Dragon picked up the treasure and attempted to climb down the curtain. This was not ideal, as curtains are designed mainly for hanging still and disappointing spiders. Halfway down, Dragon lost his grip and fell into Addin’s laundry basket, which Mum had just brought back upstairs.

“There you are,” said Mum.

“I have always been here,” said Dragon, muffled by a school shirt.

Mum lifted him out by the middle. His tail remained wrapped around the treasure.

Dad pointed at it. “I really do need that.”

Dragon held it closer.

Bob ran in at that moment carrying Biffin upside down by one leg.

“Dragon found trasure!” Bob shouted.

“Treasure,” said Addin.

“Trasure,” said Bob, with confidence.

Biffin blinked from near Bob’s knee. “Is it for sleeping on?”

Dragon regarded him with suspicion. Biffin asked simple questions, but they were often placed dangerously near the truth.

“Yes,” said Dragon. “And therefore it should not be interfered with by clock repair, laundry, dogs, or anyone who says trasure.”

Bob put Biffin on the floor. Biffin slowly righted himself, then looked at Dragon’s object.

“It looks like the bit off Dad’s clock,” he said.

Dragon’s nostrils smoked.

“That is merely because Dad’s clock resembles treasure.”

Dad nodded. “That can happen.”

Mum gave Dad a look.

Dad changed expression into one more suitable for responsible adulthood.

“Dragon, I do need to fix the alarm clock.”

“I need to sleep.”

“You slept all morning.”

“That was preliminary.”

Mum set the laundry basket down. “You can sleep somewhere else.”

Dragon looked wounded. “One cannot simply sleep somewhere else. A proper sleeping place requires assessment, arrangement, height, warmth, defensive value, and a pleasing firmness under the stomach.”

Bob nodded solemnly, though he understood only the word stomach.

So Dragon set off to find another place.

First he tried the parents’ bed, in the warm fold between two pillows. He placed the treasure beneath him, arranged one floppy arm over it, and had just begun to feel ancient and respected when Mum came in to change the sheets.

“No.”

“I am assisting by holding the mattress in place.”

“You are holding an alarm-clock cover in my clean sheets.”

Dragon was moved on.

Next he tried the airing rack in the kitchen, among dry socks and small towels. It was warm, which was excellent. It was dry, which was essential. It had height, provided nobody examined the matter closely. Dragon settled down on the treasure and began to hum faintly through his nose.

Then Mum noticed smoke.

“Dragon!”

“It is only a gentle appreciative warmth.”

“You are under the tea towels.”

“I was improving them.”

“You are not improving the tea towels with fire.”

“I did not say fire.”

A tiny spark appeared from one nostril and died against a sock.

Mum moved him on with both hands and a firm expression.

After that Dragon tried the back of the sofa in the rear living room. This had promise. It was high enough to insult Murphy from, soft enough for sleep, and near Dad, who was reading the paper and had the peaceful air of a man who would not start a laundry-related campaign without warning.

Dragon placed the treasure beneath him.

Dad lowered the paper.

“You know I can see it.”

“I know many things.”

“I still need it.”

“Later,” said Dragon.

Dad considered this. “How much later?”

Dragon closed his eye. “Historically later.”

This might have worked, because Dad was willing to accept vague arrangements when sitting down, but Bob came in with a wooden train set. Bob did not mean to disturb Dragon. Bob meant to build a tunnel across the sofa, which was an entirely separate project and only disturbed Dragon because Dragon was on the sofa at the time.

Murphy followed Bob. Biffin followed Murphy. Addin followed Biffin’s tail, which had picked up a train bridge without Biffin noticing.

Within three minutes, Dragon’s sleeping place had become a mountain, a station, a tunnel, and briefly the dangerous cliffs near the biscuit tin.

“I am not scenery,” Dragon said.

“You can be the dragon,” said Bob.

“I am already the dragon.”

“You can be the other dragon.”

Dragon sat up slowly.

There are insults so large that they become almost architectural.

Before Dragon could respond properly, Murphy put his nose on the sofa and sniffed the treasure. Dragon seized it, leapt sideways, bounced off a cushion, and landed in Dad’s lap.

Dad caught him by reflex.

“Well done,” said Addin.

“I intended that,” said Dragon, though he had intended nothing between the leap and the landing except survival.

Murphy wagged at him from the floor.

Dragon was moved on.

He tried the stairs next, settling on the broad step beneath the window that looked out over the back garden. It had sunlight. It had height from Murphy, provided Murphy remembered he was a dog and not a weather event. It had a pleasant view of the grass, which was acceptable because the grass was outdoors where wet things belonged.

Dragon put the treasure beneath him and sighed with deep satisfaction.

Then Bob needed to come downstairs.

Bob came downstairs in the manner of a small child moving furniture with his whole body. He brought Biffin, three toy cars, one sock, and a question about whether dragons could eat peas. Addin came behind him, carrying the train bridge that Biffin’s tail still had not released.

“Dragon’s on the stair,” said Addin.

“I am not on the stair,” said Dragon. “I am occupying a defensible ledge.”

“You’ll get stepped on,” said Addin.

Dragon looked at Bob’s descending feet and conceded the point privately, which is the only proper way to concede anything.

He moved.

The third bedroom seemed promising because no one used it much. It held a box of old Christmas decorations, a chair with clothes waiting to become somebody’s problem, and a faint smell of dust. Dragon liked it immediately. Rooms no one used were often full of things no one had yet noticed were treasure.

He climbed onto the chair, settled on the alarm-clock cover, and tucked his repaired ear beneath his arm. For a while the house hummed around him. Mum folded clothes downstairs. Dad searched for a missing screwdriver. Addin read. Bob explained something complicated to Biffin, and Biffin listened with the patient expression of someone still thinking about peas.

Dragon became nearly asleep.

Then Biffin came in.

He stood in the doorway and looked around slowly.

“What are you doing?” Dragon asked.

“I was following my tail,” said Biffin.

His tail, which had curled around Dad’s missing screwdriver, tapped gently against the doorframe.

Dragon narrowed his eye.

“That is not yours.”

“No,” said Biffin. “I think it is following me.”

Biffin stepped toward the chair to show Dragon the screwdriver. His foot caught the handle of a bag of Christmas baubles. The bag tipped. The baubles rolled. Biffin watched them with mild fascination. His tail lifted the screwdriver out of the way just in time, which was useful but did not improve the general rolling situation.

One bauble struck the chair leg.

The chair wobbled.

Dragon clutched the treasure.

“Do not,” he said, though he was not certain whether he was speaking to Biffin, the chair, the baubles, or the universe.

The chair wobbled again.

Dragon slid off the cushion, down the side, and into the box of Christmas decorations with a soft thump and a faint jingling sound.

Biffin peered in.

“Are you Christmas?”

Dragon’s voice emerged from tinsel. “No.”

Mum appeared at the door. “What was that noise?”

“Biffin discovered gravity,” said Dragon.

“I found Dad’s screwdriver,” said Biffin.

Dad arrived behind Mum. “Oh good.”

Dragon climbed out of the box. A loop of silver tinsel hung around his neck. It would have looked festive on almost anyone else.

Dad picked up the screwdriver, then saw the treasure again.

“Dragon.”

Dragon held it against his belly.

Dad crouched down. “I need that piece to mend the clock. But after I’ve fixed it, you can sleep on the whole clock for a bit. On the shelf. High up. No dog.”

Dragon hesitated.

This was not an unreasonable offer. It had treasure, height, warmth from the nearby radiator, and the possibility of looking important beside a functioning clock. A functioning clock had status. It ticked. It told people what to do. Dragon admired that in principle.

“For how long?” he asked.

“Until bedtime.”

Dragon looked at Addin, who had come quietly to the doorway.

“It’s a good shelf,” said Addin. “Murphy can’t reach it.”

Murphy, hearing his name, arrived with his tongue out and no useful contribution.

Dragon looked at the dog. Murphy wagged. Dragon looked at the treasure. Then he looked at Dad.

“I shall permit a repair,” he said, “under supervision.”

“That seems fair,” said Dad.

“It was my intention all along to improve the household machinery.”

“Of course.”

Mum took the tinsel off Dragon’s neck. “And not to steal the clock cover?”

Dragon drew himself up. “Borrowing treasure is a recognised dragon custom.”

“Recognised by whom?”

Dragon thought. “Dragons.”

This was difficult to disprove without widening the conversation more than anyone wanted before tea.

Dad fixed the travel alarm clock at the kitchen table while Dragon sat beside him on a folded tea towel, watching closely. The little gold-edged cover snapped into place with a click. Dragon flinched, then pretended the flinch had been inspection.

Dad wound the clock. It began to tick.

Dragon stared at it.

The clock was better now. It had been treasure before, certainly. But now it was treasure with a heartbeat.

Dad placed it on the high shelf in the living room, near the radiator and safely above Murphy’s reach. Addin lifted Dragon up. Bob lifted Biffin up too, though Biffin had not asked and looked mildly surprised to have become involved in height.

Dragon settled himself over the clock. It was not as comfortable as the loose cover had been. It made a small ticking noise under him, which at first seemed impertinent. But the shelf was warm, the dog was below, the family was nearby, and no one was carrying laundry in his direction.

Biffin sat beside him and watched the clock’s hands.

“It is moving,” he said after a long while.

“It is working,” said Dragon.

“Are you sleeping on time?”

Dragon considered this.

“Yes,” he said. “Briefly.”

Below them, Mum folded the last of the clothes. Dad found his paper again. Addin read on the sofa. Bob built a railway line that went nowhere in particular but did so with confidence. Murphy lay on the rug and dreamed of catching something soft and green-blue, though without malice.

Dragon closed his eye.

The clock ticked beneath him. It was not quite the same as sleeping on a private piece of trasure under a pile of clothes. But it was warm, elevated, repaired, and officially permitted.

For a dragon of mature judgement, that was almost better.

Almost.
