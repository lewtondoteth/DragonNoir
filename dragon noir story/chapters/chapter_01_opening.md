# Chapter 1 - Opening

Canonical current Chapter 1 draft.

This file integrates the rain/city monologue, Dragon's trifle-hangover state, the knock at the door, the pink dragon doorway reveal, mutual recognition, and the office-entry transition.

It had been raining over Toy City for three days, or possibly four.

After the second day, counting became a hobby for optimistic toys.

Rain changed the value of things.

A good coat became a bad coat. A dry match became treasure. A shortcut became a canal.

A respectable toy became something with water in his stuffing and murder in his heart.

In this office, murder had arrived before breakfast and settled in without taking off its coat.

Rain was not hated here. Hatred suggests effort. Possibly respect.

Rain was objected to.

Quietly. Continuously. On principle.

It got into things.

Into coats. Into paper. Into floorboards. Into stitching and then, somehow, further than that.

A damp toy woke up tired. Thought slower. Moved slower. Felt vaguely insulted by existence.

Toy City was full of damp toys.

Most of them had given up mentioning it.

The office contained a blanket, a desk, a dragon-shaped regret, and an empty trifle bowl.

The bowl was not entirely empty.

This presented a difficulty.

Licking it clean would have been undignified. Leaving it there was worse.

The matter had been under consideration for some time.

The rain tapped on the window.

It was ignored.

It tapped again.

Toy City moved outside in damp little pieces. Wheels hissed through puddles. Feet slapped the pavement. Somewhere below, two toys argued under an awning in the low, tired way of people who had run out of dry insults.

The blanket was pulled tighter.

The rain kept making its point.

Then someone knocked on the door.

It was a polite knock.

That made it worse.

A rude knock could be ignored on principle. A desperate knock could be ignored with regret. A polite knock stood there in the hallway wearing good manners and expecting civilisation to continue.

I waited.

The knock came again.

Still polite.

Somewhere in the office, a floorboard gave a small damp complaint. The blanket had become less a blanket than a legal argument against movement. My head contained trifle, weather, and one sharp spoon of regret.

None of these were billable.

I got up.

This took longer than standing should take. Stuffing shifts when it has been damp too often. Knees that had never been properly designed developed opinions. My coat hung over the chair-back like something that had escaped a canal and regretted surviving.

I left it there.

A private investigator should meet clients in a coat.

A private investigator should also avoid carrying half the weather indoors on his shoulders when there was already enough of it in his soul.

The office was not large, but it had arranged itself badly. The desk stood where a desk usually stands when it wishes to be in the way. Papers leaned in damp stacks. A pencil rolled underfoot and was spared only because it was too dull to be treasure.

The trifle bowl watched from beside the lamp.

"Later," I told it.

This was not a promise.

The third knock arrived just as I reached the door.

Still polite.

Still patient.

The sort of knock that had either money behind it or trouble. Experience suggested both, but never in the helpful order.

I opened the door.

Rain stood in the hallway.

Behind it stood a dragon.

For a moment the office, the weather, the bowl, the headache, and the general disappointment of the morning all stepped politely aside.

She was small.

About my size, which is to say smaller than most respectable furniture and larger than most excuses. A little rain hat sat between two neat horns. A dark coat hung from her shoulders, damp at the edges but not defeated. Beneath it, where the hallway light found her, her plush was pink.

Not bright pink.

Not new pink.

Old pink. Faded at the seams. Soft where handling had worn it down. The colour of something kept in a drawer with tissue paper and a reason no one had explained.

She had a rounded snout.

Two small teeth.

Button eyes.

Long arms.

Little paws with the same inward curve at the wrist.

The same kind of horns.

The same proportions, adjusted by time in a kinder workshop.

It is one thing to see another dragon in a book, or painted badly on a sign outside a cheap toy theatre. It is another to open your own office door and find an earlier answer to a question you had stopped asking.

She looked at me.

Not quickly.

She took in the flattened plush, the repaired eye, the wrong ear, the visible stitching, the place near my side where old stuffing tried to remember its shape. Her gaze did not flinch. That was discourteous in a very precise way.

Then she looked past me into the office.

The desk.

The papers.

The blanket collapsed on the floor where it had lost the argument with morning.

The trifle bowl catching light beside the lamp like physical evidence.

None of this improved my position.

Rain ticked from the brim of her hat onto the hallway floor.

Softly.

Carefully.

As if even the weather had lowered its voice.

She looked back at me.

"Are you Dragon?"

There were several possible answers.

Some were professional.

Some involved closing the door and returning to the blanket until the world arranged itself into something less personal.

I chose civilisation, narrowly.

"If I'm not," I said, "I should fire the fellow who painted the door."

Her mouth did not quite smile.

That was worse as well.

She stood quietly in the doorway while the rain gathered along the brim of her little hat and fell in soft ticks onto the hallway floor.

Behind me, the office waited in all its professional disappointment.

Behind her, Toy City dissolved slowly into rain.

Neither option looked especially responsible.

"You'd better come in," I said eventually.

This was less hospitality than structural concern. Enough rain gets into a hallway and the whole building starts developing opinions.

She stepped inside carefully.

Small steps.

Same as mine.

The door shut behind her with a tired little click and the room immediately felt smaller in a way the architecture had not approved beforehand.
