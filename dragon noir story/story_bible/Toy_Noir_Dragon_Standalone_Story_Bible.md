# Toy Noir Dragon — Standalone Story Bible

## Purpose

This document is a standalone story bible for a toy-noir detective story starring Dragon: a small, shabby, treasure-obsessed stuffed dragon private investigator.

Everything needed for character, world, tone, plot, and style is included here.

---

# 1. Core Story Identity

## Premise

A shabby toy dragon private investigator is hired by a mysterious pink dragon to find a missing toy connected to a legendary treasure. The case draws him through the rain-slick streets of Toy City and into a conspiracy involving stolen toy parts, reconstructed identities, staged heroism, and an ancient patchwork guardian being rebuilt for public spectacle.

Dragon begins the story wanting payment, treasure, dryness, comfort, and professional dignity.

He ends the story scarred, treasureless, wiser than he wanted to be, and alone in his office after doing the right thing in a way that no one properly recognises.

## Genre

- Toy noir detective mystery
- Living-toy fantasy
- Melancholy adventure
- Emotional mystery rather than horror
- Suitable for readers aged roughly 10 and up

## Core Promise

A small, battered toy dragon who would much rather stay dry, warm, paid, and undamaged is drawn into a mystery that forces him to choose compassion over treasure, truth over comfort, and sacrifice over instinct.

## Story World Mode

Toy Noir Dragon is a self-contained toy-noir detective story world.

Dragon is a small, shabby, treasure-obsessed stuffed dragon private investigator working in Toy City, a full living-toy metropolis with theatrical scale, handmade logic, noir atmosphere, and emotional sincerity.

The world should feel broad, confident, and theatrical. The toys are real, the city is real, and the story does not need to apologise for either fact.

Dragon is not a sleek noir hero. He is shabby, vain, treasure-minded, cowardly, dryly funny, comfort-loving, water-fearing, easily offended, physically fragile, and unexpectedly brave when it matters.

---

# 2. Tone

## Overall Tone

The tone should be:

- noir-inflected
- rainy
- wry
- emotionally grounded
- melancholy but not bleak
- funny through understatement
- atmospheric but not over-written
- mysterious but not frightening in a horror sense
- sincere beneath Dragon’s grumbling

The story should feel like a detective tale told by a battered toy who has seen too many lost buttons, too many botched repairs, and far too much rain.

## Emotional Effect

The reader should finish feeling:

- fond of Dragon
- sad for what he loses
- satisfied by his reluctant courage
- aware that he is changed
- comforted by the fact that damaged things can still act with dignity
- moved, but not crushed

The ending should be bittersweet rather than despairing.

## Humour

Humour should come from:

- understatement
- practical toy-world logic
- Dragon’s failed dignity
- noir seriousness applied to small absurdities
- rain, repairs, treasure, and office inconveniences
- calm reactions to ridiculous situations
- Dragon pretending not to care when he clearly does

Avoid forced jokes, internet humour, parody-noir overload, or constant wisecracking.

Often the funniest line is the one that stops early.

# 3. Dragon — Main Character

## Name

Dragon.

He may sometimes be called “Mr Dragon” by clients, officials, repair clerks, or toys attempting politeness.

He dislikes being called:

- little dragon
- plushie
- damp
- collectible
- cute

## Role

Private investigator in Toy City.

He specialises in:

- missing toys
- lost treasures
- disputed ownership of shiny objects
- suspicious repairs
- replacement fraud
- damaged-memory cases
- quiet toy mysteries other investigators consider beneath them

He accepts payment in coins, cufflinks, polished buttons, watch parts, rare marbles, antique keys, and occasionally actual money if absolutely necessary.

## Essential Nature

Dragon is simultaneously:

- a living being
- a stuffed toy
- a dragon
- a private investigator
- a mildly troublesome creature with a high opinion of himself

He is small, shabby, dignified, comfort-loving, opportunistic, cranky, guardedly affectionate, treasure-obsessed, fire-breathing when dry, terrified of water, and not nearly as hard-boiled as he thinks he is.

Dragon is not cruel. He may be selfish, evasive, vain, lazy, and dishonest in small ways, but he must never become malicious or mean-spirited.

## Physical Description

Dragon is approximately two feet tall when standing upright, though he rarely enjoys being measured.

He is made from faded plush fabric somewhere between green and blue, worn unevenly by age, handling, weather, and previous incidents best described by insurers as “enthusiastic animal involvement.”

Distinguishing features:

- rounded snout
- long floppy arms
- long tail
- brown fabric belly
- two small white teeth
- one visible black button eye
- one damaged or repaired eye area
- one ear reconstructed incorrectly
- visible stitching across his body
- small patches of exposed stuffing
- flattened plush
- mismatched repair thread
- occasional scorch marks near his snout when particularly annoyed

In the noir version, he often wears practical detective accessories scaled badly to his body:

- a worn coat or rain cape that is too stiff when dry and too heavy when wet
- a tiny hat he considers essential to the profession
- a notebook with damp-softened pages
- a pencil stub
- perhaps a satchel for evidence, treasure, and snacks

His clothing should never make him look genuinely cool. It should make him look like someone who has made an effort under difficult circumstances.

## Personality

Dragon is:

- observant
- suspicious in practical ways
- treasure-minded
- dryly comic
- comfort-seeking
- self-protective
- proud
- stubborn
- lazy when laziness is possible
- unexpectedly persistent when treasure, dignity, or someone vulnerable is involved
- compassionate after several layers of complaint

He has seen enough of Toy City to distrust dramatic explanations, heroic speeches, and anyone who says, “This will only take a moment.”

He is cynical in speech but not in soul. His noir manner is partly a defence against being small, old, repaired, and easy to damage.

## Strengths

Dragon’s strengths are:

- sharp observation
- practical deduction
- noticing small objects
- understanding treasure motives
- reading damage patterns
- recognising poor stitching
- negotiating with difficult toys
- persistence when offended
- ability to breathe fire when dry
- knowing how fear works
- understanding what damaged toys do to preserve dignity

He is especially good at noticing what other toys dismiss as unimportant: loose thread, missing buttons, polish residue, damp fabric, replacement stuffing, scratches on a key, or the way a toy avoids mentioning a repair.

## Weaknesses

Dragon’s weaknesses are:

- fear of water
- greed for treasure
- vanity
- physical fragility
- dislike of effort
- preference for comfort
- suspicion of dogs
- reluctance to be emotionally honest
- tendency to assume treasure is relevant, even when it is not
- tendency to keep small pieces of evidence if they are shiny

His fear of water is not only a joke. It is a real physical and emotional vulnerability.

Rain weighs him down. Dampness weakens his fire. Wet stitching makes him miserable. Being called damp makes him furious.

## Fire

Dragon can breathe genuine fire.

Fire rules:

- works best when Dragon is dry
- weakens quickly in dampness
- fails almost completely when soaked
- may emerge as sparks when annoyed
- may appear as nostril smoke when offended
- becomes stronger when he is frightened but dry
- is unreliable in rain

Dragon’s fire should be used sparingly. It is not a superhero power. It is a dangerous, practical, limited ability, often thwarted by weather, fear, poor timing, or damp cloth.

## Water

Water is Dragon’s greatest fear and recurring humiliation.

Water includes:

- rain
- puddles
- baths
- washing machines
- damp cloth
- wet pavements
- leaks
- dripping ceilings
- soggy coats
- being described as “a little damp”

Dragon does not merely dislike water. He regards it as unreasonable, unsafe, undignified, and personally insulting.

## Treasure Instinct

Dragon has a deep instinct to collect treasure.

His treasure includes:

- coins
- keys
- cufflinks
- watch gears
- buttons
- polished beads
- old toy medals
- brass screws
- lost jewellery from dollhouses
- bright scraps of foil
- anything small, hard, shiny, and morally ambiguous in terms of ownership

Treasure calms him. Sitting on treasure makes him sleepy and content.

The central emotional arc of the story depends on this instinct. Dragon must want the legendary treasure intensely enough that destroying it matters.

He should not give it up easily or nobly. He should give it up because by the end he knows exactly what it costs him and does it anyway.

## Relationship to Damage

Dragon has been repaired many times.

He does not treat damage as tragedy. He treats it as insult, inconvenience, poor service, and evidence that the world is badly organised.

Damage carries emotional weight. Dragon’s repairs are practical facts of his life, while Toy City’s repair economy and the central conspiracy make repair politically and emotionally charged.

Being torn apart at the midpoint should not be graphically horrific. It should be frightening, disorienting, and intimate. The emphasis is on loss of wholeness, vulnerability, memory, and trust.

After repair, Dragon should carry mismatched replacement parts that connect him physically to the wider mystery.

## Detective Code

Dragon does not think of himself as noble.

His code is practical:

- take the case if the fee is decent
- never trust anyone who enjoys rain
- count treasure before and after a conversation
- do not promise heroism
- do not leave small toys alone in dangerous places
- never ignore a bad repair
- always check the stitching
- do not let fear make the final decision

He would deny having a code.

This denial should not be believed.

# 4. First-Person Noir Voice

## Narration Mode

First-person past tense, told by Dragon.

The voice should be noir-inflected without becoming parody.

Dragon should sound:

- dry
- guarded
- observant
- formal in flashes
- practical
- wry
- self-important
- wounded beneath the surface
- more emotionally honest than he intends

## Voice Target

The prose should combine:

- detective narration
- understated humour
- practical toy-world detail
- melancholy atmosphere
- Dragon’s failed dignity
- child/YA readability

Dragon should narrate like someone who believes he is harder-boiled than he is.

He notices practical things first: exits, damp patches, loose seams, shiny objects, repair quality, who is lying, where the heater is, and whether there is somewhere high to sit.

## Prose Rules

Use:

- plain sentences with occasional noir rhythm
- concrete physical detail
- practical absurdity
- understatement
- dry similes drawn from toy life, office life, repair shops, rain, fabric, dogs, treasure, and cheap detective work
- emotional restraint

Avoid:

- constant wisecracks
- adult hard-boiled clichés piled too high
- “doll dame” parody language
- sexualised femme fatale tropes
- graphic violence
- grimdark cynicism
- purple prose
- modern internet humour
- moral lectures

## Dialogue Rules for Dragon

Dragon speaks in a low grumble.

His speech should be:

- mildly formal
- defensive
- theatrical when protecting dignity
- quietly sarcastic
- slower than his thoughts
- evasive about feelings

When possible, and when it does not feel artificially forced, Dragon should answer questions with statements rather than direct yes/no replies. He often responds sideways, as if the answer is obvious and the question has slightly lowered his opinion of the room. This is a tendency, not a rigid gimmick.

Example:

> “Are you Dragon?”

> “If I’m not, I should fire the fellow who painted the door.”

Examples:

> “Payment in advance is not greed. It is civilisation.”

> “I am not afraid of rain. I object to it professionally.”

> “There is a difference between damaged and finished. I have had occasion to study the matter.”

> “I did not steal the cufflink. I preserved it from a careless society.”

## Emotional Honesty

Dragon rarely says what he feels directly.

He reveals emotion through:

- noticing repairs
- protecting smaller toys
- returning treasure reluctantly
- refusing to leave someone in danger
- anger at cruelty disguised as practicality
- discomfort when someone sees his damage clearly
- silence after jokes that cut too near the truth

# 5. Toy City

## Core Setting

Toy City is a full living-toy metropolis. It is treated with complete confidence: the toys are real, the city is real, and the story does not need to apologise for either fact.

The city is not merely a hidden corner under a bed or a secret society inside a human house. It is a proper city populated by toys, with streets, offices, districts, institutions, rumours, economies, civic rituals, underworld networks, myths, and repair politics.

The world can still feel handmade, theatrical, and toy-scaled. Buildings may be assembled from toy boxes, stage flats, old shop displays, model-town pieces, dollhouse architecture, cardboard signage, tin roofs, wooden blocks, velvet curtains, railway sets, and repurposed human objects. But the city should function as a complete world, not as a hidden household secret.

## Atmosphere

Toy City is:

- rain-slick
- lamp-lit
- full of repair shops
- crowded with small lives
- emotionally worn
- funny in practical ways
- built from discarded objects
- full of toys afraid of being broken, forgotten, replaced, or rebuilt badly

The rain matters. For Dragon, every case in the rain is personal.

## City Logic

Toys live, work, trade, argue, hide, repair, remember, forget, and fear damage.

Possible city rules:

- damaged toys can be repaired but may return changed
- replacement parts carry emotional weight
- some toys remember where they came from; some do not
- repair shops function like hospitals, tailors, confessionals, and black-market clinics
- being forgotten is a social fear
- being replaced is an existential fear
- being disassembled is the closest thing to horror, but should be handled with restraint

## Humans

Humans are currently optional.

Possible approaches:

1. Humans are absent and irrelevant.
2. Humans exist outside the frame, like distant giants or weather.
3. Humans are mythical to some toys and mundane to others.
4. Humans created many of the toys but do not control Toy City.
5. Human presence is treated theatrically, with flexible scale and realism.

The default approach should keep humans out of the main plot unless they become useful.

## Districts

### The Lower Drawers

A cramped, damp district where old toys, loose parts, and forgotten things gather. Cheap repairs, worse coffee, better information.

Dragon knows the Lower Drawers well and pretends not to.

### Button Market

A crowded trading district where buttons, beads, small gears, doll shoes, marbles, and questionable evidence change hands.

Treasure is everywhere. Dragon finds this professionally distracting.

### The Repair Quarter

A row of shops, clinics, back rooms, and official stitching houses. Some are kind. Some are expensive. Some ask no questions because they already know the answers.

### Shelfside

Higher-status toy neighbourhood. Cleaner fabric, better polish, fewer visible repairs, more lies.

The pink dragon may come from here or pretend to.

### The Lost-and-Found

A municipal office, warehouse, or mythic archive where missing toys are catalogued badly.

No one fully trusts it.

### The Feltworks

Industrial zone where stuffing, cloth, wire, thread, and replacement parts are processed. Could be tied to the conspiracy.

### The Hearth District

A warmer, older area beloved by fire-capable toys, mechanical toys, and those who dislike damp. Dragon prefers it but cannot afford to work there.

### The Rain Gutters

Dangerous channels and alleys where water gathers. Dragon avoids them until the plot makes that impossible.

## Institutions

### Repair Shops

Repair shops are central to Toy City.

They function as:

- hospitals
- tailors
- mechanics
- memory clinics
- gossip exchanges
- black-market part suppliers
- places of care
- places of exploitation

Good repair preserves identity.

Bad repair erases, replaces, or repurposes it.

### The Civic Toy Council

A respectable public body that claims to maintain order and protect toys.

Some members may be involved in the staged heroism conspiracy.

### The Hero Office / Public Safety Bureau

A civic institution responsible for public rescues, ceremonies, and city morale.

This is where manufactured heroism may be staged.

### The Stitchers’ Guild

Official repair authority. Some members are compassionate. Others are complicit. Many are afraid.

### Treasure Brokers

Dealers in old objects, shiny things, dragon hoards, and artifacts.

They understand Dragon better than is convenient.

# 6. Pink Dragon

## Role

Client, mirror, temptation, emotional foil, and compromised guide into the conspiracy.

She occupies the femme fatale story function in a 10+ toy noir world.

She should be mysterious without being sexualised, manipulative without being cruel, and emotionally real beneath her lies.

## Name

Currently unresolved.

Possible names:

- Rose
- Rosette
- Pink
- Pearl
- Mallow
- Coral
- Felicity
- Miss Rosebutton
- Miss Rosethread
- Clementine, if the pink is faded toward peach

She may initially refuse to give her real name.

Dragon might call her “Miss Pink” in narration until he learns it.

## Physical Description

She is another rare toy dragon, close enough to Dragon’s type that their connection matters.

She is made from faded pink plush.

Features:

- soft pink fabric, worn at the edges
- careful repairs, much neater than Dragon’s
- button eyes
- pale belly fabric
- delicate but not fragile in spirit
- one patch hidden beneath a ribbon, scarf, or collar
- stitching that suggests expensive repair work
- perhaps a faint scent of cedar drawers, old perfume, or stored fabric

She should not seem glamorous in an adult way. Her glamour is toy-world glamour: rare fabric, careful stitching, clean seams, controlled fear, and the impression that she has spent a long time learning how not to look damaged.

## Personality

She is:

- intelligent
- controlled
- observant
- self-interested
- sneaky
- persuasive
- rare and aware of it
- brave in a colder way than Dragon
- manipulative when it suits her
- genuinely capable of care
- especially drawn to Dragon because they are both rare toy dragons

She sees through Dragon quickly.

She recognises his treasure instinct, his vanity, his fear, and his kindness.

This makes him uncomfortable.

## Relationship with Dragon

Dragon is drawn to her immediately because:

- she is another dragon
- she is rare
- she understands treasure
- she seems damaged in a hidden way
- she treats him seriously
- she frightens him a little

Her interest in Dragon begins as practical. He is useful. He is a dragon. He may be able to find, handle, or understand the artifact. But her interest becomes genuine. Part of this is because they are both rare toy dragons. She recognises something in him: age, damage, stubborn dignity, and the lonely absurdity of being a rare creature in a city full of replaceable ones.

Their relationship should be emotionally charged but restrained.

They do not need grand declarations.

A better emotional beat is Dragon noticing that she is limping and pretending not to, or her drying his sleeve without commenting on it.

## Moral Position

The pink dragon is sneaky, self-interested, and connected to the conspiracy or the artifact trail in ways she does not reveal at first.

She is not simply a villain, and she is not merely a helpless client.

Her first instinct is to protect herself and get what she wants. She is a dragon, and therefore treasure has a pull on her too. She understands Dragon’s greed because she shares a version of it.

Possible versions:

1. She wants the artifact for herself, then discovers the cost is worse than she expected.
2. She is using Dragon because only another dragon can find or handle the treasure.
3. She is involved with the conspirators but plans to betray them.
4. She is trying to rescue someone while pretending to pursue treasure.
5. She thinks Dragon is useful at first, then finds herself caring about whether he survives.

Her care for Dragon should be genuine but compromised.

She manipulates him because she wants something. She grows attached because he is Dragon.

## Arc

She begins as a client withholding information.

She becomes a suspect.

She becomes someone Dragon wants to save despite knowing she has used him.

Her self-interest should never disappear entirely. She does not become sweet, pure, or made safe by affection. Instead, she becomes more complicated: still sneaky, still capable of betrayal, but no longer able to treat Dragon as only a tool.

She leaves at the end.

Her departure should hurt without becoming melodrama.

Possible final note:

She leaves behind a small non-magical treasure, perhaps a pink thread, a button, or the unpaid part of his fee.

Dragon keeps it and insists this is only because it may become evidence later.

No one should believe him.

# 7. Central Mystery

## Opening Case

A mysterious pink dragon hires Dragon to find a missing toy connected to a legendary treasure.

Dragon takes the case because:

- she offers treasure
- she is another dragon
- she knows more than she says
- the rain outside makes refusing seem sensible, but the fee makes refusing difficult
- he is immediately drawn to her in ways he disguises as professional suspicion

## Initial Missing Toy

The missing toy should be emotionally specific, not just a plot token.

Possible candidates:

1. A small patchwork knight toy who once guarded the artifact.
2. A wind-up mouse courier carrying the artifact’s last known location.
3. A cracked porcelain child-doll with a memory stitched inside.
4. A little bear from the Lost-and-Found who saw too much.
5. A cloth bird who can identify stolen parts by touch.

This remains open.

## Escalation

Dragon finds the missing toy torn apart or badly damaged.

The damage is strange:

- specific parts are missing
- seams are opened cleanly
- stuffing is taken selectively
- replacement thread is present
- the pattern suggests harvesting, not random violence

More cases appear.

Toys are not merely being hurt. They are being used.

## Pattern

The stolen parts come from special toys with particular qualities:

- bravery from a knight
- memory from an old bear
- grace from a dancer
- loyalty from a dog toy
- voice from a singing doll
- strength from a wooden soldier
- fire-resistance or dragon-thread from Dragon’s kind

The conspiracy is rebuilding something from symbolic and physical parts.

## Central Conspiracy

A secret faction is reconstructing an ancient legendary toy guardian from stolen toy parts.

Their plan:

1. Rebuild the guardian.
2. Unleash it in a controlled public catastrophe.
3. Arrange for a chosen hero to defeat it.
4. Restore public faith in the city’s institutions.
5. Consolidate power through fear, gratitude, and myth.

The conspiracy believes Toy City has become frightened, divided, cynical, and hard to govern.

They want a miracle they can manage.

They are wrong about the “manage” part.

## The Chosen Hero

The chosen hero is a public-facing toy intended to defeat the guardian in front of the city.

Possible forms:

- a golden action figure with a perfect smile and hollow courage
- a ceremonial clockwork knight
- a celebrity rescue bear
- a newly manufactured dragon promoted as the city’s protector
- a stitched-together heroic mascot built for publicity

The chosen hero should not be purely evil. They may be vain, manipulated, frightened, or unaware of the full truth.

# 8. The Artifact

## Current Status

The artifact is not yet finalised.

It must be a legendary treasure that is especially irresistible to dragons, not just generically valuable.

It should connect emotionally to one or more of the following:

- comfort
- hoarding
- memory
- warmth
- dryness
- control
- identity
- being whole
- being rare
- being safe

Destroying it should stop or free the guardian, but should also cost Dragon something instinctive and personal.

## Possible Artifact Directions

### The Ember-Stone

A small warm treasure that lets a dragon feel perfectly dry, warm, safe, and comfortable.

This is the strongest current candidate if the story’s emotional centre is Dragon giving up comfort, warmth, treasure, and safety.

Why it works:

- directly tempts Dragon
- connects to his fear of water
- connects to his fire
- gives him something he would desperately want
- makes the final sacrifice concrete and painful

Possible function:

The Ember-Stone can calm the Patchwork Guardian by giving it warmth and unity, but the conspirators use it to control the guardian. Dragon must destroy it to break that control.

### The First Button

A golden button from the first dragon toy ever made.

This is strong if the story’s emotional centre is rarity, dragon identity, and connection between Dragon and the pink dragon.

Why it works:

- deeply meaningful to dragons
- connects Dragon and the pink dragon
- makes the treasure about identity
- symbolises being rare rather than replaceable

Possible function:

The First Button binds dragon-thread and can animate or control reconstructed toys. Destroying it breaks the guardian’s binding.

### The Mirror Bead

A polished bead that shows a toy as whole, no matter how damaged they are.

This is strong if the story’s emotional centre is repair, shame, and identity after damage.

Why it works:

- reflects Dragon’s repaired body
- reflects the guardian’s patchwork body
- gives damaged toys the comfort of seeing themselves whole
- tempts Dragon emotionally rather than only materially

Possible function:

The Mirror Bead makes the guardian believe it is whole, but also traps it in a false identity. Destroying it lets the guardian see what has been done to it.

### Other Options

- The Brass Key: opens any locked toy chest, drawer, or memory box
- a tiny crown that makes every toy recognise its bearer as important
- The Music-box Gear: soothes the guardian by playing its original lullaby
- a polished coin that always returns to the owner’s hoard

## Recommended Direction

The current recommended artifact is the Ember-Stone.

It gives Dragon the clearest sacrifice: he must destroy the one object that promises everything he instinctively wants — warmth, dryness, treasure, comfort, and safety.

# 9. The Patchwork Guardian

## Identity

The Patchwork Guardian is an ancient legendary toy protector reconstructed from stolen parts.

It should feel sad before it feels frightening.

It is not evil. It is wronged, incomplete, confused, overburdened, and built for a purpose it did not choose.

## Physical Description

The guardian may be enormous by toy standards, perhaps human-child sized or larger.

It is assembled from:

- plush limbs
- wooden joints
- tin plates
- doll eyes
- mismatched stuffing
- knight armour scraps
- music-box gears
- thread from many repairs
- dragon-resistant cloth
- old heroic symbols

It should look awe-inspiring and pitiable at once.

## Emotional Function

The guardian represents:

- repair without consent
- resurrection as control
- myths rebuilt for politics
- the danger of forcing damaged things to become symbols
- the difference between being mended and being used

## Behaviour

When unleashed, the guardian does not behave like a monster.

It behaves like something overwhelmed by too many parts, memories, commands, and expectations.

It may:

- pause at familiar sounds
- protect one toy while endangering others
- repeat old guardian phrases
- respond to the artifact
- become distressed by fire
- become confused by being called a hero or monster

The climax should make readers feel the tragedy of it.

## Ending for the Guardian

Current rough ending:

- Dragon destroys the artifact to free the guardian from control.
- The guardian falls into fire or is consumed by fire.
- Its destruction is sad but necessary.
- A small remnant remains: a button, patch, or piece of thread.

This should be handled with emotional restraint.

# 10. Supporting Cast

## Repairer Ally

A practical repair-shop owner who has stitched Dragon before and knows too much.

Possible forms:

- an elderly teddy bear with spectacles sewn on crookedly
- a tin nurse doll
- a sock monkey surgeon
- a wooden mannequin tailor
- a calm rag doll who never wastes thread

Function:

- explains repair politics
- warns Dragon
- repairs him after midpoint attack
- recognises stolen-part patterns
- cares without sentimentality

## Information Broker

A toy who trades in gossip, objects, and lost things.

Possible forms:

- magpie toy
- wind-up rat
- marble-eyed clown
- jack-in-the-box who knows things because everyone forgets he is listening

Function:

- gives leads
- tempts Dragon with treasure
- offers comic suspicion

## Public Hero

The manufactured hero meant to defeat the guardian.

Should be more pathetic than villainous.

Possible emotional angle:

They know they are not brave but have been told the city needs them. Their public polish hides private terror.

## Conspiracy Leader

A respectable civic figure who believes controlled myth is necessary.

Possible forms:

- porcelain mayor doll
- senior Stitchers’ Guild official
- antique soldier
- clockwork judge
- museum curator toy

They should not cackle. They should explain themselves in reasonable terms.

Their danger lies in thinking toys are safer when someone else controls the story.

## Smaller Missing Toys

The damaged or missing toys should each have enough specificity to matter.

They are not just clues. They represent possible futures for Dragon.

# 11. Story Structure

## Act I — The Client in the Rain

- Dragon is in his office, avoiding rain and unpaid bills.
- Pink dragon arrives.
- She hires him to find a missing toy and/or artifact.
- Dragon notices she is lying by omission.
- He accepts anyway, because treasure and curiosity are old enemies of good judgement.
- First leads take him into Toy City’s lower districts.

## Act II — Torn Seams

- Dragon finds evidence of damaged toys.
- Missing toy is found torn apart or badly harvested.
- Dragon discovers specific parts are being taken.
- Repair shops are frightened.
- The pink dragon’s story begins to crack.
- The legendary treasure becomes more real.
- Dragon finds or recovers the artifact at midpoint.
- He is attacked.
- He is torn apart / badly damaged.
- He is repaired using mismatched replacement parts.
- His body now connects him to the conspiracy.

## Act III — The Wrong Hero

- Dragon investigates from a more personal place.
- He learns about the staged catastrophe.
- The chosen hero is revealed.
- Pink dragon’s involvement becomes clear.
- Dragon feels betrayed but continues.
- The guardian is completed.
- The city prepares for a public heroic event, not knowing it is manufactured.

## Act IV — Fire and Treasure

- The guardian is unleashed.
- The planned heroism fails.
- The guardian cannot be controlled as expected.
- Pink dragon is endangered.
- Dragon has the artifact and wants to keep it.
- He realises the artifact must be destroyed to stop/free the guardian.
- Destroying it goes against his deepest dragon instinct.
- He does it anyway.
- Fire, rain, and repair all converge.
- The guardian is destroyed or released.
- The conspiracy is only partly exposed.

## Ending — Office Alone

- Public story credits someone else.
- The city accepts a cleaner version of events.
- Pink dragon leaves.
- Dragon returns to his office.
- He is physically and emotionally scarred.
- He has no legendary treasure.
- He has perhaps one small ordinary object left behind.
- Rain continues, or stops just enough to be annoying in a different way.

The ending should be quiet, dry, and bittersweet.

# 12. Themes

## Obsession vs Compassion

Dragon wants treasure because he is a dragon. The story should honour the strength of that instinct rather than treating it as a simple flaw.

His sacrifice matters because wanting the treasure is real.

## What Makes Someone Whole

Dragon’s body is repaired, mismatched, and visibly damaged. The guardian is reconstructed from many parts but not truly whole.

The story asks whether wholeness comes from original parts, memory, care, consent, or choice.

## Repair vs Resurrection

Good repair helps a toy continue being itself.

Bad reconstruction uses pieces of toys to create a symbol, weapon, or public myth.

Dragon’s own repair history gives him moral authority here, though he would resent being told that.

## Manufactured Heroes and Myths

The conspiracy creates danger in order to sell safety.

The staged hero is meant to restore belief.

Dragon becomes the real hero precisely because he does not want to be one and receives little credit.

## Letting Go

The final act requires Dragon to give up the treasure he most wants.

He does not become less dragon by doing this.

He becomes more himself.

Do not state this as a moral. Let the action show it.

# 13. Forbidden Drift

Do not let the story become:

- adult noir parody
- grimdark toy horror
- action-movie fantasy
- epic dragon mythology
- superhero adventure
- cynical political allegory without heart
- romance-forward melodrama
- a story where Dragon becomes cool and fearless
- a story where damage is treated graphically
- a story where repair equals simple healing
- a story where the pink dragon is merely deceitful
- a story where Dragon’s treasure instinct disappears
- a story where Dragon becomes malicious or cruel
- a story where the guardian is just a monster
- a story where all mysteries are over-explained

Do not overuse noir slang.

Do not make Dragon sound like a 1940s detective pasted onto a plush toy. The noir should be in the weather, rhythm, suspicion, moral compromise, loneliness, and first-person perspective — not just in vocabulary.

# 14. Style Examples

## Office Opening

The rain had been falling since Tuesday, which was unreasonable of it.

I watched it crawl down the office window in silver threads and tried not to take it personally. Water and I have never had a professional relationship. It gets into the stitching, weakens the fire, and encourages people to say things like “you’re only a little damp,” which is how civilisation begins to fail.

I was sitting on three coins, a broken watch chain, and a cufflink I had no intention of returning, when she came in.

Pink plush. Button eyes. One careful patch along the neck. Too clean for the lower drawers, too frightened for the upper shelves.

“Are you the detective?” she asked.

I let a little smoke curl from my nostrils. It was mostly for effect.

“That depends,” I said. “On the case, the fee, and whether anyone expects me to go outside.”

## Dragon on Payment

There are detectives who work for justice. There are detectives who work for truth. I have always admired them from a sensible distance.

I work for payment.

This is not because I lack principles. It is because principles do not shine when stacked under one’s stomach during an afternoon nap.

## Dragon on Rain

Rain makes philosophers of some people. It makes poets of others.

It makes me damp.

This is one of the many reasons I have never trusted poetry.

## Dragon on Repair

The repairer turned my left arm over twice and made a noise through her stitches.

Repairers have several noises. This was not one of the cheerful ones.

“How bad?” I asked.

“That depends,” she said, “on whether you were attached to this seam emotionally.”

“I have never been attached to anything emotionally,” I said.

She looked at the cufflink under my tail.

“That,” I said, “is different.”

## Dragon on the Pink Dragon

She was pink in the way old roses are pink after someone has pressed them inside a book and forgotten why they meant something.

That was the sort of thought I disliked having. It suggested the case was already becoming expensive.

## Dragon on the Guardian

It came through the smoke in pieces.

Not broken pieces. That would have been easier. Broken things know what they are.

This had been mended by someone who did not understand the difference between repair and hunger.

# 15. Current Open Questions

These are the main creative decisions still unresolved.

## Artifact

1. Is the artifact definitely the Ember-Stone, or should it be the First Button, Mirror Bead, or something else?
2. Does destroying it free the guardian, destroy the guardian, break control over the guardian, or all three?
3. Was the artifact originally made for dragons, for the guardian, or by accident?

## Pink Dragon

4. What is her name?
5. Did she know of Dragon before she came to his office?
6. Is she working for the conspirators, against them, or using both sides?
7. Does she leave because she must, because she chooses to, or because staying would require honesty?

## Toy City

8. Are humans absent, mythical, distant, or simply outside the frame?
9. Are all toys alive automatically, or only some?
10. How theatrical should the city feel compared with how physically logical it should be?

## Damage Level

11. How frightening can the damaged/disassembled toys be for a 10+ audience?
12. Should the midpoint damage to Dragon be shown directly, implied, or discovered after blackout?
13. How visibly altered should Dragon be after repair?

## Ending

14. Does the public ever learn the truth?
15. Who wrongly receives credit?
16. What small object, if any, does the pink dragon leave behind?
17. Does the final scene end with rain, warmth, smoke, or silence?

# 16. Ready-to-Use Drafting Prompt

Use this standalone story bible as the authoritative reference for a toy noir Dragon detective story.

Write in first-person past tense from Dragon’s point of view. Preserve Dragon’s shabby dignity, treasure instinct, fear of water, dry humour, practical intelligence, physical fragility, guarded affection, and reluctant courage. Use a noir detective atmosphere without slipping into parody, grimdark, adult hard-boiled clichés, or graphic horror.

The story should be melancholy but suitable for readers aged roughly 10 and up, with humour coming from understatement, toy-world practicalities, Dragon’s failed dignity, rain, repair logic, treasure obsession, and emotional restraint.

Story premise:

A mysterious pink dragon hires Dragon, a toy private investigator, to find a missing toy connected to a legendary treasure. The case reveals a conspiracy to reconstruct an ancient patchwork guardian from stolen toy parts and stage a public act of heroism. Dragon must ultimately destroy the treasure he most desires in order to save the pink dragon and stop or free the guardian.

Before finalising, check that:

- Dragon remains consistent: shabby, vain, treasure-minded, cowardly, dryly funny, comfort-loving, water-fearing, easily offended, physically fragile, and unexpectedly brave
- the noir voice remains readable and restrained
- the damage is emotionally serious but not graphic
- the pink dragon is complex rather than simply deceitful
- the artifact matters emotionally to dragons
- the guardian is tragic rather than monstrous
- the ending is bittersweet rather than bleak
