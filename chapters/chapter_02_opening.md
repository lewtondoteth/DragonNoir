# Chapter 2 Opening

We moved through the legal formalities.

Sign here. Initial there. Standard confidentiality. Standard authority to act. Standard language confirming that if the case became expensive, unpleasant, or difficult to explain, I would still be paid for standing too close to it.

She signed without fuss.

That should have pleased me. All it did was make me wish I had asked for more.

We agreed my usual rate. Expenses separate. Payment terms standard. She accepted the figure with a small nod, as if money were one of those practical details other people became emotional about.

"Thank you, Mr Dragon," she said.

I capped my pen, gathered the papers, and turned to the filing cabinet.

The middle drawer stuck. I worked it open, filed the forms, and shoved it closed.

When I turned back, Odette was gone.

No goodbye. No footsteps. Just the empty chair, turned slightly toward my desk, and the faint trace of perfume in the rain-damp air.

The case, apparently, had begun on credit.

I opened the catalogue again.

Harrow & Vale had put their address at the bottom of the page in small, confident print. They did not need larger print. Everyone in Toy City knew where Harrow & Vale stood, the way everyone knew where the police station stood, where the best repair rooms refused credit, and which tea shop watered the milk.

The place was not hidden.

That made it either convenient or expensive. In my experience, the two conditions often introduced themselves together and left separately.

The auction began at seven-thirty.

My head reminded me, with the sincerity of a public notice, that sherry trifle was not a breakfast food, a dinner food, or a wise professional choice under any known category. I ignored it. A private investigator has to choose between comfort and progress several times a day, and comfort had been making poor arguments since noon.

I started with the notebook.

It was under a folded receipt, behind the cold cup, where I had placed it with the clear intention of not losing it. The office had a talent for taking a thing left unattended and moving it six inches into obscurity. Given a full afternoon, it could misplace a filing cabinet.

My purse had fallen between the desk and the wall. I retrieved it with a ruler, a hook, and language I would not have used in front of a paying client.

My identification was in the second drawer, underneath three unpaid bills and a button I did not remember acquiring. The button went into my purse. Evidence should be preserved.

The keys were where keys always are after five minutes of looking: exactly where I had checked first, but with a worse attitude.

The umbrella was by the door.

I considered leaving it there. Umbrellas are optimistic instruments. They begin every outing with a promise and end it turned inside out in a gutter, explaining nothing.

I took it anyway.

Outside the window, the rain continued its shift. It had settled into the kind of weather that did not fall so much as apply itself. The glass trembled under the larger drops. The sill had given up pretending to be dry.

I put on my coat. It was stiff in the shoulders and damp along the hem, which meant it had achieved the standard professional balance between defence and betrayal. My hat came next. Then the satchel, notebook, purse, identification, keys, and umbrella, each one checked twice because the office was watching.

I turned down the lamp, locked the filing cabinet, and stood for a moment with one paw on the door handle.

Behind me, the office held its breath in the warm, paper-heavy gloom.

Ahead of me, Toy City waited under water.

I locked the door.

By the time I reached the street, the rain had found the weak places in my coat, my hat, my left ear, and my general theory of justice.

I opened the umbrella.

It opened.

Dragon one.

Rain six hundred and five.
