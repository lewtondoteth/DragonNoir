# Chapter 1 - Opening

Canonical current Chapter 1 draft.

This file integrates the rain/city monologue, Dragon's trifle-hangover state, the knock at the door, the pink dragon doorway reveal, office-entry transition, coffee sequence, and layered recognition progression.

It had been raining over Toy City for three days, or possibly four.

After the second day, counting became a hobby for optimistic toys.

Rain changed the value of things.

A good coat became a bad coat. A dry match became treasure. A shortcut became a canal.

A respectable toy became something with water in his stuffing and murder in his heart.

In this office, murder had arrived before breakfast and settled in without taking off its coat.

Rain was not hated here. Hatred suggests effort. Possibly respect.

Rain was objected to.

Quietly. Continuously. On principle.

It got into things.

Into coats. Into paper. Into floorboards. Into stitching and then, somehow, further than that.

A damp toy woke up tired. Thought slower. Moved slower. Felt vaguely insulted by existence.

Toy City was full of damp toys.

Most of them had given up mentioning it.

The office contained a blanket, a desk, a dragon-shaped regret, and an empty trifle bowl.

The bowl was not entirely empty.

This presented a difficulty.

Licking it clean would have been undignified. Leaving it there was worse.

The matter had been under consideration for some time.

The rain tapped on the window.

It was ignored.

It tapped again.

Toy City moved outside in damp little pieces. Wheels hissed through puddles. Feet slapped the pavement. Somewhere below, two toys argued under an awning in the low, tired way of people who had run out of dry insults.

The blanket was pulled tighter.

The rain kept making its point.

Then someone knocked on the door.

It was a polite knock.

That made it worse.

A rude knock could be ignored on principle. A desperate knock could be ignored with regret. A polite knock stood there in the hallway wearing good manners and expecting civilisation to continue.

I waited.

The knock came again.

Still polite.

Somewhere in the office, a floorboard gave a small damp complaint. The blanket had become less a blanket than a legal argument against movement. My head contained trifle, weather, and one sharp spoon of regret.

None of these were billable.

I got up.

This took longer than standing should take. Stuffing shifts when it has been damp too often. Knees that had never been properly designed developed opinions. My coat hung over the chair-back like something that had escaped a canal and regretted surviving.

I left it there.

A private investigator should meet clients in a coat.

A private investigator should also avoid carrying half the weather indoors on his shoulders when there was already enough of it in his soul.

The office was not large, but it had arranged itself badly. The desk stood where a desk usually stands when it wishes to be in the way. Papers leaned in damp stacks. A pencil rolled underfoot and was spared only because it was too dull to be treasure.

The trifle bowl watched from beside the lamp.

"Later," I told it.

This was not a promise.

The third knock arrived just as I reached the door.

Still polite.

Still patient.

The sort of knock that had either money behind it or trouble. Experience suggested both, but never in the helpful order.

I opened the door.

Rain stood in the hallway.

Behind it stood another dragon.

The office, the weather, the bowl, the headache, and the general disappointment of the morning all stepped politely aside.

Not for long.

My head was in no condition for miracles before breakfast.

She was small.

About my size, which is to say smaller than most respectable furniture and larger than most excuses. A little rain hat sat low between two neat horns. A dark coat hung from her shoulders, damp at the edges but not defeated. Beneath it, where the hallway light found her, her plush was pink.

Not bright pink.

Not new pink.

Old pink. Faded at the seams. Soft where handling had worn it down. The colour of something kept in a drawer with tissue paper and a reason no one had explained.

The coat hid most of her shape.

The hat shadowed the rest.

Still.

Dragon.

She looked at me.

Not quickly.

She took in the flattened plush, the repaired eye, the wrong ear, the visible stitching, the place near my side where old stuffing tried to remember its shape. Her gaze did not flinch. That was discourteous in a very precise way.

Then she looked past me into the office.

The desk.

The papers.

The blanket collapsed on the floor where it had lost the argument with morning.

The trifle bowl catching light beside the lamp like physical evidence.

None of this improved my position.

Rain ticked from the brim of her hat onto the hallway floor.

Softly.

Carefully.

As if even the weather had lowered its voice.

She looked back at me.

"Are you Dragon?"

There were several possible answers.

Some were professional.

Some involved closing the door and returning to the blanket until the world arranged itself into something less personal.

I chose civilisation, narrowly.

"If I'm not," I said, "I should fire the fellow who painted the door."

Her mouth did not quite smile.

That was worse as well.

She stood quietly in the doorway while the rain gathered along the brim of her little hat and fell in soft ticks onto the hallway floor.

Behind me, the office waited in all its professional disappointment.

Behind her, Toy City dissolved slowly into rain.

Neither option looked especially responsible.

"You'd better come in," I said eventually.

This was less hospitality than structural concern. Enough rain gets into a hallway and the whole building starts developing opinions.

She stepped inside carefully.

Small steps.

A pause before each one.

Toe first, then weight, then the rest of the body following after the floor had proved it could be trusted.

The door shut behind her with a tired little click.

Damp worked softly through the crack above the radiator.

I had stopped noticing it sometime around yesterday.

She hadn't.

One paw disappeared briefly into the opposite sleeve.

The coat shifted slightly at the shoulders afterward and settled again.

I looked toward the window.

Water had gathered along the sill in a thin reflective line. The radiator beneath it hissed weakly to itself while the draft wandered through the office making separate enemies of both warmth and dignity.

I crossed the room and shoved the window closed.

The latch caught.

Immediately the office felt smaller.

The rain lowered its voice against the glass.

It was built for two desks and not much forgiveness.

Mine had spread gradually over the last several months into a condition difficult to classify professionally.

Blanket.

Papers.

Three cups.

A kettle.

One pillow leaning sideways against the filing cabinet with the tired posture of something no longer entirely convinced it belonged anywhere else.

The radiator clicked twice behind me.

The far side of the office remained intact beneath the second lamp.

Chair pushed in.

Blotter square.

Drawers closed properly.

A hat still hanging from the coat stand nearby exactly where it had been left, which was becoming statistically impressive.

Dust had gathered there carefully.

I took my coat off the client chair before the room could continue introducing itself.

A file slipped sideways from the corner of the desk and landed quietly on the floor.

Then another.

Professional collapse rarely travels alone.

I picked up the top one and returned it to the nearest pile, where it immediately became somebody else's problem.

Behind me, she moved further into the office.

Small steps.

Measured around the desk leg, the loose file, the place where the floor dipped near the radiator.

Her tail followed a half-second later, avoiding the same trap mine had found three mornings out of five.

That was beginning to become distracting in a way I distrusted immediately.

"Sit down," I said.

The chair creaked faintly beneath her as she folded into it in one neat economical movement. One paw settled near the edge of the desk afterward beside the other in exactly the sort of arrangement my own usually arrived at during difficult conversations.

The room continued shrinking around the issue.

For a few seconds neither of us said anything.

The rain filled in professionally where conversation had failed.

She looked once toward the kitchenette against the far wall.

Then back at me again.

Not pointedly.

Just noticing the room.

The coffee machine sat beside the sink beneath a row of hanging cups with one button worn smooth from long service.

Civilisation, against considerable evidence, remained available.

"Coffee?" I asked.

"Please," she said.

Good.

Coffee gave a room terms.

I crossed to the kitchenette, collecting the trifle evidence on the way.

One beside the sink.

One on the windowsill.

A third under yesterday's newspaper, where it had taken shelter from consequence.

I stacked them beside the kettle with the labels turned inward.

This improved nothing.

The labels were not the charge. The trifles were.

I had been persecuted by custard before and survived with my reputation largely theoretical.

Behind me, the chair creaked once.

The coffee machine waited beneath the hanging cups. Red plastic. Silver sticker. One button worn pale from long service.

I took down two cups.

Placed the first beneath the spout.

Pressed the button.

"Chk-tchk-tchk-tchk."

"That's a cappuccino noise."

"Yes."

A pause.

"That's an americano cup."

Rain moved softly against the window.

Right.

"Chk... tchk-tchk. Chk... tchk-tchk."

Another pause.

"Better."

The cup filled beneath the little plastic nozzle.

I moved the second cup underneath.

"Chk... tchk-tchk."

One of the files on the desk slid quietly onto the floor.

The radiator clicked twice.

I poured the first cup.

"Glug."

Then the second.

"Glug."

Too much in the first.

Naturally.

I kept that one.

When I carried hers across the office, she took it in both paws.

Careful paws.

Pink plush. Neat seams. The same small inward turn at the wrist.

Close enough.

I sat behind the desk.

The chair considered the matter and declined formal complaint.

Rain moved down the window in thin uneven lines.

"Glug."

The coffee reached the headache first.

Then the older places.

Across the desk, she lowered her cup onto the saucer without sound.

One paw was already waiting for it before the other let go.

Steam moved between us.

Her tail slipped once against the chair leg and settled there afterward in a careful curve that left enough room for the coat behind her.

Under the desk, mine caught briefly against one of the crossbars.

I pulled it free.

The radiator clicked softly behind me.

She shifted slightly in the chair.

Not much.

One shoulder dipping a fraction while the balance changed underneath her. A paw touched the chair arm briefly. The movement ended there.

Clean.

Practiced enough to disappear if you were not looking directly at it.

I adjusted my own weight a moment later when the stuffing near my left hip started objecting to the angle.

The chair answered with a quiet creak.

Across the desk, nothing answered hers.

Rain continued working at the window.

She lifted the cup again.

Small pause before the sip.

"Glug."

I looked down at my own cup.

Steam against the snout first.

Then:

"Glug."

The coffee had quieted enough of the morning for smaller things to become audible.

A loose thread near her cuff had been trimmed close instead of pulled.

The left sleeve of my coat carried the same repair in darker thread.

Outside, rain.

Inside, steam.

Across the desk, coat sleeves, careful paws, small horns beneath the brim of the hat.

And underneath all of it, the same little negotiations with gravity.

She lowered the cup.

For a few seconds, the room held its breath in the ordinary way rooms do when they have been badly furnished and asked to contain too much.

Then she reached up and removed the little rain hat.

The horns were neat.

Not larger than mine.

Not smaller.

Neater.

She set the hat on her lap and unfastened the dark coat with two careful movements.

The coat came away from her shoulders.

Pink plush underneath.

Rounded snout.

Button eyes.

Long arms.

Little paws.

The same shape.

Not the same colour. Not the same finish. Not the same sort of life stitched into the seams.

But the same kind of dragon.

Mine was faded green-blue, flattened by weather, patched where the world had got careless.

Hers was pink.

Clean pink.

Shelf pink.

The sort of pink that looked as if dust had never found it, rain had never touched it, and no careless hand had ever squeezed too hard.

Different answers.

Same pattern.

The thought did not arrive cleanly.

It worked through the coffee, the old aches, the damp places, and the little curve of her paw around the cup.

Then it sat there.

Across from me.

Dimly, somewhere beyond the shock, rain tapped against the office window with the quiet persistence of something that had mistaken me for a personal grievance.

Then, slowly, the world started forcing its way back in.

The radiator clicked once behind the filing cabinet.

A car hissed through standing water somewhere below the office.

One of the loose papers near my elbow shifted slightly in the draft from the window frame.

The coffee had gone cooler near the top.

My tail was wound tight around one of the chair crossbars hard enough to pull at the stitching near my hip.

Thoughts arrived afterward.

Not in order.

Coffee.

Client.

Dragon.

Staring.

I had been staring.

There are ways to recover professional dignity after staring at a client. None of them had survived contact with the last ten seconds.

She did not mention it.

Her mouth moved into the smallest possible smile, then stopped before it became impolite.

"Your name," I said.

It was not my finest sentence.

"Odette."

The name settled between us with the steam.

Polished. Old-fashioned. Arranged.

It suited her well enough to make me suspicious of it.

"Miss Odette," I said, because procedure was a useful thing to hide behind when the rest of the room had become unreliable.

She lifted the cup again.

There was the same small pause before the sip.

Paws steady.

Tail placed where a careless chair could not catch it.

"Odette will do."

Naturally.

I picked up my pencil stub.

It had rolled half an inch toward the edge of the desk during the silence, possibly from sympathy.

"Then you came to the right door by accident or design," I said. "I charge differently for each."

"Design."

"That costs more."

"I expected it would."

She set the cup down, one paw waiting for the saucer before the other let go.

The sound did not arrive.

Some toys spend money on repair. Some spend it on silence.

"You find things," she said.

"When they are missing."

"And when people prefer they stay that way."

I looked at her over the pencil.

Rain tapped twice against the glass and took its time about it.

"That is a different rate."

"And discreetly."

There it was.

Not a request. A narrowing of the hallway after I had already stepped into it.

I had intended to ask what she wanted found. I had intended to ask who had sent her, how she had found the office, why she had come through three days of rain in a coat that treated weather as something that happened to other people.

Instead I opened the notebook.

The page was clean except for an old coffee mark in the corner and a note reminding me never to trust a doll with matching gloves.

"Private recovery," I said.

Odette lowered her eyes to the notebook.

"Yes."

"No police toys."

"Not yet."

"Not yet costs more than no."

"I know."

She knew too many things in too few words.

The pencil waited.

So did she.

Somewhere in the room, the trifle bowls remained unhelpfully visible.

I wrote the date.

Then I wrote her name beneath it.

OdEttE.

The letters looked too clean for my handwriting.

"Start at the beginning," I said.

She looked toward the rain, then back at me.

"Beginnings are rarely that helpful, Mr Dragon," she said. "I would rather start with the theft."

I made a note.

The pencil dragged through the damp page and left a grey trench instead of a word. The paper wrinkled under it, puffed slightly, and began absorbing the case before I had agreed to take it.

I shifted the notebook until I found a drier corner.

There was not much of one.

The note remained legible by the standards of my profession.

ThEfT.

I looked at it.

The letters looked back with the stubborn confidence of creatures that knew what shape they were meant to be and had chosen otherwise.

Odette noticed.

Of course she noticed.

"You write in a particular hand," she said.

"It discourages forgery."

"It discourages reading."

"Then it serves two purposes."

I tapped the pencil against the word.

"What was stolen?"

Her paws stayed around the cup.

"Something special to me."

I waited.

She did not improve the answer.

"Special covers a lot of floor," I said.

"In this case, enough."

The cup turned slightly between her paws. Not nervous. Measured. Like she was choosing which part of the truth could afford light.

"It was stolen," she said, "not in the legal sense. In the moral one."
