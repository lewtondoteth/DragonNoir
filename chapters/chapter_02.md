# Chapter 2 Opening

We moved through the legal formalities.

Sign here. Initial there. Standard confidentiality. Standard authority to act. Standard language confirming that if the case became expensive, unpleasant, or difficult to explain, I would still be paid for standing too close to it.

She signed without fuss.

That should have pleased me. All it did was make me wish I had asked for more.

We agreed my usual rate. Expenses separate. Payment terms standard. She accepted the figure with a small nod, as if money were one of those practical details other people became emotional about.

"Thank you, Mr Dragon," she said.

I capped my pen, gathered the papers, and turned to the filing cabinet.

The middle drawer stuck. I worked it open, filed the forms, and shoved it closed.

When I turned back, Odette was gone.

No goodbye. No footsteps. Just the empty chair, turned slightly toward my desk, and the faint trace of perfume in the rain-damp air.

The case, apparently, had begun on credit.

I opened the catalogue again.

Harrow & Vale had put their address at the bottom of the page in small, confident print. They did not need larger print. Everyone in Toy City knew where Harrow & Vale stood, the way everyone knew where the police station stood, where the best repair rooms refused credit, and which tea shop watered the milk.

The place was not hidden.

That made it either convenient or expensive. In my experience, the two conditions often introduced themselves together and left separately.

The auction began at seven-thirty.

My head reminded me, with the sincerity of a public notice, that sherry trifle was not a breakfast food, a dinner food, or a wise professional choice under any known category. I ignored it. A private investigator has to choose between comfort and progress several times a day, and comfort had been making poor arguments since noon.

I started with the notebook.

It was under a folded receipt, behind the cold cup, where I had placed it with the clear intention of not losing it. The office had a talent for taking a thing left unattended and moving it six inches into obscurity. Given a full afternoon, it could misplace a filing cabinet.

My purse had fallen between the desk and the wall. I retrieved it with a ruler, a hook, and language I would not have used in front of a paying client.

My identification was in the second drawer, underneath three unpaid bills and a button I did not remember acquiring. The button went into my purse. Evidence should be preserved.

The keys were where keys always are after five minutes of looking: exactly where I had checked first, but with a worse attitude.

The umbrella was by the door.

I considered leaving it there. Umbrellas are optimistic instruments. They begin every outing with a promise and end it turned inside out in a gutter, explaining nothing.

I took it anyway.

Outside the window, the rain continued its shift. It had settled into the kind of weather that did not fall so much as apply itself. The glass trembled under the larger drops. The sill had given up pretending to be dry.

I put on my coat. It was stiff in the shoulders and damp along the hem, which meant it had achieved the standard professional balance between defence and betrayal. My hat came next. Then the satchel, notebook, purse, identification, keys, and umbrella, each one checked twice because the office was watching.

I turned down the lamp, locked the filing cabinet, and stood for a moment with one paw on the door handle.

Behind me, the office held its breath in the warm, paper-heavy gloom.

Ahead of me, Toy City waited under water.

I locked the door.

By the time I reached the building door, the rain had found the weak places in my coat, my hat, my left ear, and my general theory of justice.

I opened the umbrella.

It opened.

Dragon one.

Rain six hundred and five.

The main door of my office building opened straight into the alley.

This was not an architectural accident. It was a statement about rent.

The alley received me with both gutters overflowing. Water came down the walls, off the fire escapes, out of the broken drainpipes, and from places where water had no obvious right to be. My umbrella took the first assault with a hollow, fabric drumbeat and immediately began reconsidering its career.

Above Toy City, noon was trying to happen. You could tell because the sky had improved from black to charcoal.

Between the buildings, a narrow strip of daylight had got itself wedged in and was making the best of a poor situation. It fell across the dumpsters in a pale line, bright enough to show the dents, the rust, and the little tin figure sitting underneath an awning made from a biscuit tin lid and two bent teaspoons.

He had a faded parade jacket, rust in both knees, and one trouser leg ending in nothing at all. His beret lay upside down in front of him, dark with rain around the brim.

I stopped.

The figure looked up. One side of his face had been painted with a moustache by someone who had meant well and stopped there.

"Afternoon," he said.

"Technically."

His joints gave a small metallic creak as he adjusted himself under the shelter. The beret waited between us.

I had principles about charity. Most of them concerned receiving it. Giving it was more complicated, especially before lunch and in weather that had already declared war on my stuffing.

I took a metal disk from my purse and dropped it into the beret.

It landed with a clean little clink.

The tin soldier nodded once. Professional. No fuss. Toy City still had standards in the alleys, though it kept them under leaking roofs.

I walked on past the dumpsters.

Three steps later, the string went tight.

I gave it a careful tug, and the disk came sliding back through the rainwater, over the cracked paving, and into my waiting paw.

Behind me, the tin soldier watched it go.

"Times are hard," I said.

"So I see."

I put the disk back in my purse and kept walking.

I felt bad for a while. Three steps. But the reality was, the only real difference between us was that I had a door behind me and a landlord willing to believe I'd pay for it eventually.

Mercer Street began where the alley gave up.

The streetlamps were already lit, which said less about the time than it did about the weather. Their yellow light stretched across the flooded road in long, trembling pieces. Anything wanting to cross Mercer Street that afternoon needed to be a boat or a horse on extremely high stilts.

I was neither, so I kept to the shopfronts.

Halfway along, between a repair room that had closed for lunch in 1912 and a tobacconist that claimed not to sell tobacco, stood a small corner shop.

It was a corner shop in the same way a thimble is a ballroom if enough people are disappointed. It sold newspapers, paper straws, boiled sweets that had lost confidence in themselves, bootlaces, cheap polish, cigarettes from under the counter, and other things nobody admitted buying until they needed them.

I did not have an account there.

We did not get on.

The bear behind the counter was large, brown, and had more stuffing than sense, which was not that difficult, to be fair. He looked like he had been open since early morning and had nothing to show for it except damp pawprints, three complaints, and a personal grievance against the bell above the door.

The bell announced me anyway.

The bear looked up from a newspaper.

"No credit."

"Good afternoon to you too."

"No credit, Dragon."

"I heard you the first time. It was unpleasant then."

I took a packet of paper straws from the counter display and laid two small coins beside it. The bear looked at the coins as if they had arrived without references.

"Buying something?"

"I enjoy surprising people."

He took the coins.

I opened one of the papers and read the front page while he watched me with the expression of a bear who had seen civilisation and filed a complaint.

MAYOR DEFENDS EXPENSES AFTER SECOND CEREMONIAL TEAPOT PURCHASE.

POLICE DENY LOSING GETAWAY CART; SAY IT WAS "MISPLACED IN AN ACTIVE DIRECTION."

REPAIR GUILD WARNS AGAINST UNLICENSED BUTTON SWAPS.

DUCKS DEMAND CLARITY ON FLOOD POLICY.

"This isn't a library," the bear said.

"No," I said. "Libraries have chairs."

I picked out a straw, put one end between my teeth, and let a thin thread of smoke escape through it. The paper warmed, yellowed, and bent slowly in the middle.

The bear stared.

"That's a bad habit."

"Most habits worth keeping are."

The straw gave up and folded over my paw. I dropped it into the ashtray by the till. The bear did not smoke, but he kept the ashtray for customers, arguments, and evidence.

I looked back at the mayoral account story.

Expenses.

A magic word.

Not quite as powerful as abracadabra, but with the added advantage of not making you sound like an idiot when you said it.

"I need to use your telephone," I said.

The bear folded his arms.

"You buying a telephone?"

"I'm buying straws."

"You bought straws."

"Then I am a customer in good standing."

The bear considered this legal argument with the care it deserved, which was none, then reached beneath the counter and produced an ancient black telephone with opinions about the future. He set it down between the boiled sweets and the polish.

"Local call."

"Naturally."

"Short."

"Professionally."

I opened the back pages, found a cab company that advertised dry seats and reasonable rates, and dialled before the bear could remember he disliked me more than he liked money.

Below the cab advertisement, a smaller article had been squeezed beside a reward notice.

Harrow & Vale burglary.

Several lots missing.

Reward offered for information leading to recovery.

The cab company said ten minutes.

I hung up the telephone and pushed it back across the counter.

The bear grunted, took it away, and disappeared briefly beneath the till.

Then he looked at me.

I looked back.

He continued looking at me.

There are contests a sensible investigator learns not to prolong. The bear had one eye more than I did and therefore a natural tactical advantage.

Eventually I bought the paper and hid behind the front page like a professional.

Ten minutes later, the cab pulled up outside.

I almost willingly ran out to it.

Almost.

The driver drove one-handed, loose and automatic, like the cab already knew the route better than either of us did.

I reached into my coat, produced the notebook, the folded catalogue, and the newspaper, and balanced the lot against my knee while the cab rattled through the rain.

The baby doll stared at me for a second.

Then he sniffed and adjusted himself behind the wheel with wounded dignity.

Blue police lights flickered across the rain ahead.

I looked up from the notebook.

The auction house emerged through the wet glass a moment later, all pale stone, tall windows, and expensive lighting, with two police cars sitting outside beneath the awning like bad news given municipal funding.

The cab slowed.

I straightened slightly in the seat.

That wasn't ideal.

Rain hammered against the roof while the blue lights pulsed softly across the windshield and over the driver's round plastic face.

The meter clicked to a stop.

I was still watching the entrance when I reached forward automatically with a handful of notes and coins, handed them through beside the partition, and started opening the door.

The driver looked down at the money.

Then back at me.

"That a tip?"

I glanced at him distractedly.

"Hm? Oh. Yeah." I pushed the door open. "And as a general life guideline, I'm usually more willing to give money to toys who spend less time being an arsehole."

The baby doll stared at me in deep personal offence.

"Get outta the cab."

"I was already leaving."

"Yeah? Then leave harder."

I stepped out beneath the rain and slammed the door shut behind me.

The cab tore away from the curb fast enough to splash gutter water across my shoes while the driver yelled something obscene out the window about my parentage, profession, and probable emotional development.

I adjusted my coat against the rain and looked properly toward the source of the blue lights.

They weren't centred on the auction house entrance.

They were clustered farther down the street instead, concentrated around one section of pavement near the mouth of a narrow service alley running alongside the building.

That definitely wasn't ideal.

Rain flashed blue-white across wet stone while police cars sat at awkward angles around the cordoned area. A line of yellow DO NOT CROSS tape sagged between portable barriers and a bent parking meter, fluttering weakly in the weather.

Half a dozen officers stood around it in various stages of damp exhaustion. A tired teddy-bear sergeant near the tape was trying unsuccessfully to keep rain off a clipboard while two little green army-men officers argued beside a camera unit barely larger than they were. Nearby, a tin soldier policeman stood rigidly beneath the awning with the fixed posture of somebody who had learned professionalism from a factory mould and never improved from there.

Nobody looked happy.

Nobody at a police scene ever does, but this had a particularly expensive flavour of misery attached to it.

I slowed slightly as I approached.

Beyond the tape I could see a shape lying near the alley wall beneath a stained grey covering sheet that the rain kept trying to peel back from one corner.

A pair of polished shoes stuck out beneath it.

Well.

At least I wasn't the only one having a bad day.

I started toward the auction-house entrance, angling around the nearest police car.

A small painted box sitting beside the barrier suddenly sprang open beside me with a violent metallic twang.

A jack-in-the-box policeman shot upward from inside it and thrust out both little gloved hands.

"Crime scene," he announced sternly, trying to shove me backward despite being approximately one-third of my size.

I reached into my coat and flipped open the PI badge.

"Private investigator," I said. "I'm here about the auction."

The jack-in-the-box squinted suspiciously at the badge, then at me, then back at the badge again, bobbing up and down on his spring the entire time as though the effort of reading physically required momentum.

"I don't care."

Then a tired voice from somewhere behind the tape called out:

"Let him through."

A pair of figures emerged from beyond the tape and started toward me through the rain.

One was a small bean-filled gecko in a fedora and trench coat considerably nicer than mine. Better stitching. Better hat. Better tailoring. The kind of coat that implied either police salary corruption or recent divorce compensation.

The other detective was one of those heavy-bottomed wobble toys built so they always rolled upright again no matter what hit them. Wide frame, tiny feet, immovable posture. He walked with the slow certainty of something that had literally never lost an argument with gravity.

The jack-in-the-box immediately sank a little lower into his box as they approached.

The gecko squinted at me through the rain.

"Dragon?"

I sighed quietly.

"Goldfoot."

"What are you doing here?"

I looked around at the police cars, the tape, the alley, and the cluster of officers standing in the rain.

"Well," I said, "I saw a large concentration of police around a DO NOT ENTER tape and thought: oh, big sales."

"Ha ha. Very funny," Goldfoot said without visible enthusiasm. "What, you trying to get your hands on the reward money for recovering the stolen auction pieces?"

Reward money.

Huh.

Well, that certainly would have been useful information to receive several hours earlier.

I made a quiet mental note to become annoyed at my client about that later.

"Believe it or not," I said, "some of us occasionally perform investigative work without first smelling cash from three streets away."

Goldfoot snorted.

The wobble-toy detective beside him continued staring at me with the steady immovable calm of a toy specifically engineered to survive stairs.

I nodded toward the alley.

"So what's all this about?"

Goldfoot folded his arms inside the expensive trench coat and gave me a thin smile.

"Perpetrators always return to the scene of the crime," he said.

I glanced toward the alley, then back at him.

"And how do we know he's the perpetrator?"

Goldfoot shrugged.

"Well," he said, "he returned to the scene of the crime, didn't he?"

Beside him, the wobble-toy detective spoke up in the deep patient tone of somebody explaining something he considered extremely straightforward.

"Our investigators believe the auction-house door was forced with a crowbar," he said.

He pointed toward the covered body in the alley.

"And he had a crowbar on him."

I looked toward the sheet again.

"Well," I said, "that certainly narrows the suspect pool down to every toy in the city who's ever unsuccessfully attempted home repairs."

"Any leads?" I asked.

Goldfoot jerked a thumb back toward the police cars.

"The tech boys are running him through the system now," he said. "We should have a name soon."

Near the curb, three small educational-computer toys were clustered around a chunky plastic laptop with glowing coloured buttons and a tiny green display screen. One of them hit the keyboard with exaggerated concentration while another kept feeding identification cards into a slot that looked suspiciously unrelated to the machine's actual design.

Every few seconds the laptop made a cheerful little beep completely inappropriate for homicide work.

I watched them for a moment.

"Efficient," I said.

"One of them passed a training course."

"Which one?"

Goldfoot shrugged.

"Still under investigation."

I looked back toward the auction-house entrance.

Honestly, there wasn't much for me out here except rain, police sarcasm, and a dead burglar with expensive tailoring.

"Any chance I can have a look around inside?" I asked.

Goldfoot snorted.

"Knock yourself out. This'll probably be solved in no time."

I left them to the rain and crossed toward the entrance.

I knocked.

The door made a noise, reluctantly. It had been hoping for better knuckles.

I stood there in the rain while the gargoyles pretended not to look at me and looked at me anyway.

Eventually, somewhere inside, a lock gave up.

The door opened a crack.

I took the crack as an invitation and stepped through it.

The toy on the other side was a nutcracker, polished to within an inch of monarchy. Red coat. Gold braid. Tall hat. A jaw built for ceremony, nuts, and disapproval.

He looked me over from hat brim to shoe leather.

"The auction house is closed, sir."

"I guessed," I said. "There's a fellow lying on your pavement making this place seem a touch unwelcoming."

Something tightened around his eyes. The sort of reaction rich places trained into their staff very carefully: not horror, not grief, just a desperate wish for unpleasant things to happen further away.

I handed him my card.

"I'm an investigator," I said. "I hear you've had a burglary."

The nutcracker took my card as if I had handed him something damp.

Which, to be fair, was the case.

Water was already dripping from my coat onto a carpet thick enough to make each drop feel like vandalism.

The nutcracker’s eyes flicked to the dark little marks spreading beneath my shoes.

His jaw tightened with a small wooden clap.

After a moment, he returned to reading the card.

Apparently the carpet could be cleaned.

Standards, once lowered, were harder work.

He lifted the card slightly closer to the light.
